MODEL & ASSUMPTIONS

A small XFEM experiment

Cracks now choose their path. A strike can start a crack near the impact or advance existing crack tips. At each growth step, the solver compares straight ahead and turns of ±25° and ±50°. It selects the direction with the largest energy release relative to the local fracture resistance.

Move the hammer to change the loading. The load stays at your selected height; it is no longer snapped to an old crack. Every active crack can respond, so a different impact can turn a path or initiate a separate crack. One strike can produce up to three successive growth steps.

Compare uniform and varied material. Varied toughness gives the specimen fixed, smooth stronger and weaker regions. This illustrative field enters the growth calculation; it is not random animation. Use the Toughness map to see it. Uniform toughness removes that variation. Straight growth can still be a reasonable result in a symmetric, uniform specimen.

What is computed. The Python solver uses Mode III (out-of-plane shear) XFEM: a scalar elastic displacement field, shifted Heaviside enrichment across each crack, and square-root tip enrichment. Each trial direction requires a new elastic solve. The left edge is fixed; a pair of opposite shear loads on the right edge approximates the hammer's effect using a 0.5 mm stopping distance.

Current limits. This is an unvalidated quasi-static teaching model, not dynamic glass shattering. Paths advance leftward and can curve, but a single tip does not split into branches. Cracks stop near an outer boundary or another crack, with a numerical cutoff of 0.15 element widths. That cutoff is not a solved final break or moving-fragment calculation. There is no plasticity, fatigue, crack-face contact, or interior-impact nucleation. Finite trial directions and notches remain mesh dependent.
